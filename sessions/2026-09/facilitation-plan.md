## 2-Minute Summary (for those who didn't read)

The article explores why software developers are particularly prone to feeling like frauds and argues that the solution lies in systemic organizational change rather than individual coping mechanisms. Because technology evolves constantly, developers face immense pressure to continually learn new skills, often off the clock, while working in isolated or remote environments that make it hard to gauge realistic peer progress. Research by Dr. Cat Hicks reveals a stark mismatch in tech workplaces: while essential developer work involves debugging, understanding complex codebases, and sharing knowledge, management primarily rewards visible outputs like written lines of code and commits. This environment leads developers to divest from vital "invisible" work, exacerbating feelings of isolation and self-doubt. Ultimately, the article argues that labeling these struggles strictly as individual "impostor syndrome" unfairly shifts the blame onto developers for broader workplace failures, such as unsupportive team cultures, bias, and misaligned incentives. To truly address the issue, organizations must take collective responsibility by incorporating continuous, incremental learning into paid working hours, rewarding knowledge sharing, and fostering inclusive, supportive environments. #### Question 0 — What is imposter syndrome?

Is imposter syndrome even real? How do you define success? If you don't reach that specific success, does that make you a failure? What do you do in your every day job that helps you cope with the constant changing demands of being a code writer? What if you don't write any code at all; what does that make you?!
- How do race, gender, class, education, and other social identities or demographic factors figure into our feelings of imposter syndrome?

---

#### Question 1 - Self-reflection

If you've ever dealt with imposter syndrome or feeling like a fraud, where did those feelings come from? Were you able to reflect on what barriers and circumstances may have led to a poorer performance outcome than anticipated? What happened, and where did you seek out answers or solutions for how to persevere?

---

#### Question 2 — Solutions

How does one find solutions to a seemingly internal issue that feels outward facing to employers and colleagues? Let's consider institutional challenges and systemic issues vs. personal failings. How can we find the balance we need? How can we shift our values and understanding of self and the field?

## Facilitator Guidance for Breakout Groups

*This section is especially for [Simon | Beaudry] running the Zoom group.*

**Before the session:**
- Have the discussion questions open and ready to screen share.
- Have a simple notes doc open (HackMD, Google Doc) to capture highlights for the share-out.
- Know the 2-minute summary well enough to deliver it conversationally.

**During the breakout:**
- Deliver the 2-minute summary first.
- Use the questions as a guide, not a script — let the conversation flow.
- **Watch the chat.** On Zoom, quieter participants often type instead of speaking. Invite them in: "I see Jamie added something in the chat — Jamie, do you want to say more about that?"
- If someone dominates, thank them and open it up: "Great point — let's hear from a few others on this."

**For the in-room group (Simon):**
- Use the whiteboard if it helps to capture ideas visually.
- Make eye contact around the table to invite quieter participants in.

**Two minutes before reconvene:**
- Wrap up the current thread.
- Ask: "What's the one thing you'd most want to share with the other groups?"
- Jot down your 2–3 highlights — you'll be sharing them in the full-group debrief.
